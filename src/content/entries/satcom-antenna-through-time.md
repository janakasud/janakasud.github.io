Historically, satellites primarily used parabolic reflector (dish) antennas as the standard high-performance solution, often with multiple feeds or mechanical despinning/steering. Simpler omnidirectional, horn, and helical designs were also common in the early years. The sophisticated multi-beam phased-array antennas used on today’s advanced satellites represent a significant technological shift from those earlier designs.

## Early Satellites (Late 1950s – Early 1960s)

The first satellites relied on basic, low-gain antennas:

- **Omnidirectional or near-omnidirectional antennas** (simple dipoles, whips, or monopoles). These radiated roughly equally in all directions. Sputnik-1 (1957) used whip antennas; early experimental communication satellites followed a similar approach.
- Slightly more advanced versions appeared on Telstar (1962), which used arrays of waveguide radiators arranged around the equator to create a broader but still relatively wide beam pattern suited to its spinning design.

These low-gain antennas were sufficient when satellites were experimental, power was limited, and links were to large, high-gain ground stations.

## Rise of Directional Antennas (Mid-1960s Onward)

As communication satellites matured (especially geostationary ones like the Intelsat series), higher gain and focused beams became essential. The dominant technology was the **parabolic reflector antenna** (the classic “dish”).

- A curved metallic reflector focuses radio waves onto (or from) a feed horn at the focal point. This produces a narrow, high-gain beam pointed at Earth (or a specific region).
- On spin-stabilized satellites, these were often **mechanically despun** so the beam stayed pointed at Earth while the satellite body spun.
- On three-axis stabilized satellites, the dishes were fixed or could be mechanically steered.
- Multi-beam versions used multiple feed horns illuminating a single large reflector to create several spot beams.

Other common types included:

- **Horn antennas** — simple, flared waveguides that provided moderate gain and were often used as feeds for larger reflectors or for broader coverage.
- **Helical (helix) antennas** — used for circular polarization and moderate gain, especially in telemetry, tracking, and command (TT&C) links or certain mobile satellite services.

Large deployable mesh reflectors later appeared on some high-power satellites to achieve even higher gain while fitting inside the launch fairing.

## Transition Toward Arrays

Phased-array and array-fed antennas existed in limited forms earlier (especially in military or experimental satellites), but full **electronically steerable phased-array antennas** became practical and widespread only in more recent decades.

These antennas allow a satellite to form and rapidly steer multiple independent beams purely by electronic phase control, without mechanical movement. This capability is especially valuable for modern LEO constellations that must track many moving user terminals simultaneously.
