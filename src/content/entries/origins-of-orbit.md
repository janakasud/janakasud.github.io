Long before artificial satellites orbited Earth, visionaries across centuries laid the physical, literary, and engineering foundations.

In **1687**, **Sir Isaac Newton** established the fundamental physics that would later make artificial satellites possible in his *Principia Mathematica*. He later illustrated the idea of orbital motion through the cannonball thought experiment in *De mundi systemate* (*A Treatise of the System of the World*), published posthumously in 1728.

![Newton's cannonball thought experiment](/images/articles/the-origins-of-orbit/newtons-cannonball.svg)

Through his famous **“Newton’s Cannonball”** thought experiment, he showed that a projectile launched horizontally from a sufficiently high mountain, at sufficient velocity, could continuously fall around Earth without touching the ground, effectively entering orbit as a theoretical satellite.

*Figure 1. Newton’s cannonball thought experiment, from A Treatise of the System of the World (1728). Public domain. Source: Wikimedia Commons.*

By **1869**, the concept moved into literature. American author **Edward Everett Hale** published ***The Brick Moon*** in the *Atlantic Monthly*, describing a giant brick satellite launched into Earth orbit to serve as a visual navigational aid for ships at sea.

At the turn of the 20th century, science became reality. In **1903**, Russian pioneer **Konstantin Tsiolkovsky** published the mathematical foundations of rocketry, deriving the fundamental rocket equation and proving that artificial Earth satellites and space stations were scientifically achievable. Two decades later, in **1923**, German rocketry pioneer **Hermann Oberth** elaborated on orbiting spacecraft and manned space stations in *The Rocket into Interplanetary Space*.

The crucial bridge to modern satellite communications arrived in **1928–1929**, when Austro-Hungarian engineer **Herman Potočnik** (writing as **Hermann Noordung**) published *Das Problem der Befahrung des Weltraums* ("The Problem of Space Travel"). Potočnik provided detailed engineering drawings for a habited space station in **geostationary orbit** and described using radio for Earth communications.

In **October 1945**, building upon these historical foundations, **Arthur C. Clarke** published his landmark article **“Extra-Terrestrial Relays”** in *Wireless World*. Clarke, explicitly citing Potočnik's earlier work, asked a simple question:

> **“Can rocket stations give world-wide radio coverage?”**

His answer was yes. Clarke described how artificial satellites placed in geostationary orbit—**35,786 kilometres above the equator**—would remain fixed over the same point on Earth, relaying television, telephone, and radio signals globally.

Just twelve years later, on **October 4, 1957**, theoretical vision finally became engineering reality. A team of engineers led by chief designer **Sergei Korolev** launched **Sputnik 1** aboard an R-7 rocket from the Baikonur Cosmodrome. An 83.6 kg polished aluminum sphere equipped with four whip antennas, Sputnik 1 entered an elliptical Earth orbit and began broadcasting its iconic "beep-beep" radio signal on 20.005 and 40.002 MHz. It proved the fundamental possibility of artificial orbital flight, studied the density of the upper atmosphere, and officially ushered humanity into the Space Age.

What began with Newton's equations and Hale's fiction evolved through the theoretical blueprints of Tsiolkovsky, Potočnik, and Clarke before Korolev's team put the first man-made object into orbit. Today, those early pioneering steps form the foundation for global communications and Earth observation infrastructure.
